## Detailed Problem

Seventeen Indian retailers sell the same transistor. One lists it as `BC547`, another as
`BC547 Transistor (Pack of 5)`, a third as `BC547 Transistor – NPN – TO-92 – CDIL`. Each
quotes a price per piece, per pack or per reel without saying which, so none of the three
prices can be compared to another. Multiply that across 184,801 listings and nobody can tell
an engineer what a transistor costs.

Components101, an Indian electronics-components publisher, commissioned a federated search
layer over those retailers. Making the prices comparable is an entity-resolution problem:
decide which listings refer to the same physical component. The obvious key is the
manufacturer part number. **The obvious key does not work — a third of this catalog does not
have one.**

Finding that out took building the pipeline first, and it was not the only decision the data
overturned. Three of this system's central choices were made confidently, documented, and then
contradicted by a measurement: how identity is keyed, which stores could be reached from
where, and how category should influence ranking. Each section below takes one of them and
follows the same four beats — what was decided, what the measurement showed, what changed, and
where it stands now.

## Entity Resolution

### First reversal — the part number cannot carry identity

**Decided:** identity is an exact match on the normalized manufacturer part number. Nothing
fuzzy, no functional grouping.

**Measured:** against the real corpus, **36.3% of listings — 67,128 of 184,801 rows — carry
no part number at all**, far beyond the modules-and-kits exception the design allowed for. A
single part number turned out not to mean a single product either: `BMP585` resolves to four
physically different items, from a bare IC to two breakout boards at roughly twice and four
times the price. And variant suffixes cut the opposite way to the normalization rule —
`ESP32-WROOM-32D` against `-32E` is identity, not noise to strip.

**Changed:** resolution was deferred, and the key was left strict. Relaxing the match to cover
the missing third would have merged genuinely different parts, and that error is the expensive
one — `BC547`, `BC547A` and `BC547B` are three different gain bins, so a false merge puts a
wrong price on a real component. A duplicate row costs a reader a second glance; a false merge
costs them the wrong part. For listings with no part number, identity moves to a
human-assigned identifier that behaves exactly like a real one.

**Where it stands:** that turns a matching problem into a review queue with a cost per row,
and the size of that queue — 67,128 rows — is now the thing to optimize. The engine was built
and delivered to Components101 and runs on their internal infrastructure; it has not been
released, and the team that took over is still integrating it.

### What the strict key still buys

Escalation, not guessing, is what makes the strictness workable. An exact hash match handles
listings that carry a part number; embedding similarity handles the rest within blocks keyed
on category and manufacturer; anything landing between the auto-merge and auto-separate
thresholds goes to human review, or for genuinely ambiguous pairs to an LLM adjudicator.
Blocking is what makes that affordable — pairwise comparison across a corpus this size runs
to roughly seventeen billion pairs, and blocking holds each comparison set in the hundreds.

Known clone pairs are the one sanctioned exception: `SS8050` and `8050`, `LM7805` and `L7805`
are mapped in an explicit alias table, enumerated and never inferred.

## Engineering Decisions

### Second reversal — reachability belongs to the IP, not the store

**Decided:** three stores were classified as bot-protected and reachable only from a
residential network, and the deployment plan placed them there.

**Measured:** the first full fleet run put all three on a datacenter host instead, and all
three completed successfully. The original finding had measured one datacenter address's
reputation and attributed it to the stores. The inverse held too — a plain WooCommerce store
with no protection history turned out to be datacenter-blocked, and a store fingerprinted as
one platform was running another.

**Changed:** the three cleared stores moved to the datacenter host and the blocked one moved
to residential, inverting the original placement. The decision record was amended in place
with the contradicting evidence rather than quietly corrected — the reasoning had been sound
and only its scope was wrong, and a record showing both is worth more than one showing only
the conclusion.

**Where it stands:** two stores still require a residential path, and every classification is
treated as a measurement with an expiry rather than a property of the store.

### Third reversal — a ranking signal must amplify, never gate

**Decided:** in the search harness, category classification entered ranking as an additive
bonus, on the assumption that knowing a query's category should promote matches in it.

**Measured:** the bonus was large relative to the term rewarding retrieval position, so one
wrong category label could bury an excellent match. Two strong matches for a module query,
ranked fifth and fourteenth by combined keyword and vector retrieval, fell to 496th and
1,548th once the category term was applied.

**Changed:** a bounded multiplicative boost replaced it. Category can now lift an
already-good match above its neighbors but can never push a poorly-retrieved item above a
well-retrieved one. Classification will never be perfect at this scale, so it cannot be
allowed to act as a gate.

**Where it stands:** the bounded form is what the public beta runs on.

## Architecture

Every one of those measurements needed a corpus to measure against, and building it was most
of the work. Seventeen storefronts, twelve of them on three shared transports and five on
their own.

Each store implements two methods against a shared abstract base: `discover()`, which a
master process calls to seed crawl items, and `process()`, which workers call per item.
Throttling, snapshot persistence, error logging and run accounting live in the base, so
onboarding a retailer is two methods and a parser. That abstraction is the one central
decision measurement *confirmed* rather than overturned: when ten further retailers were added
at once, a probe of each candidate's homepage, platform API paths and HTML fingerprints sorted
eight of them into the existing bases, leaving two that needed bespoke code.

A master/worker system runs over `pgmq`, one queue per store, with workers competing through
`FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`. Masters are long-lived for a single scrape and own run lifecycle;
workers run continuously and attach to whichever run is active, so masters come and go beneath
a stable pool. Keeping the queue inside the transactional database means an item and the rows
it produces commit together, which removes a class of partial-failure states an external
broker introduces. One Postgres instance carries the relational data and the queue; the vector
extension ships in the image for the resolver's embedding tier but is not yet enabled.

### The search harness

A deliberately throwaway harness came first, to find out whether the taxonomy and query
interpretation could locate the right parts before the production stack committed to a
retrieval design. It indexed 132,884 classified products — an earlier seven-store dataset,
before the fleet reached seventeen — combined keyword and vector retrieval through reciprocal
rank fusion, and used an LLM to turn each query into a category and a search keyword. It was
good enough to justify the approach, and Components101 deployed it as a public beta, where it
picked up user registration, retention and click analytics, a photo-identification endpoint
and a browser extension. It is still the throwaway stack.

## Workflow

Most operational pain in a scraping fleet comes from runs that end in an ambiguous state, so
run status is classified from the database rather than from in-loop counters that are racy
under a worker pool. Only non-recoverable losses count: dead-lettered items and parse
failures. A transient error that retried and succeeded leaves the run clean, and an HTTP 404
on a stale sitemap link is recorded as not-found and is not a loss at all — that is store-side
link rot, not a pipeline fault.

That distinction is what makes the status field worth reading. Without it every run on a fleet
this size looks degraded, and an operator stops checking.

## Outcomes

All seventeen storefronts run end to end, and the first full fleet run captured 184,801 raw
product listings. The bot-protected store contributed well over half of them through a
browser-driven warm-up flow executed from a residential host.

The scraper layer carries 397 tests, all on inline fixtures with no database or network. That
boundary is also their limit, and a five-pass review found what it leaves uncovered: a
deduplication check that ran before insert with no database constraint behind it, workers that
could attribute output to a finalized run, and a malformed queue message that crash-looped
workers without dead-lettering. Each is a database or queue behavior that an inline-fixture
suite cannot see. All were fixed and covered by regression tests.

The review also measured something no checklist would have caught: four stores had no
politeness delay on the product path, and the one publishing a `Crawl-delay` of five seconds
was being fetched a second apart. All four now sleep between fetches, and that store honors
the full five seconds.

## Limitations

The resolver is built but not in the search path, so the beta still compares listings rather
than components — the capability the whole identity model exists to provide is not yet what
its users experience. Closing that gap means working through a queue sized by the 67,128 rows
with no part number, and that cost has not been paid.

Two stores depend on a residential network path. That is an operational dependency, not a
solved problem: a change in either store's protection posture reopens it. The proxy-rotation
layer that would mitigate it is built and wired but unused, so it is untested under load.

The engagement ended in July 2026 with the resolver delivered and the fleet running, and the
system has continued under a different team since. What transfers is the habit rather than the
figures: every number here is a measurement of one system on one day, this project was wrong
about three of them, and the ones that have not been re-measured are the ones to distrust
first.
